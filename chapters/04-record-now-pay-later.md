# Record Now, Collect Later

The cooperative sees its bank balance fall when it buys paper. It sees a positive job result weeks later, before the customer pays. Someone calls this double counting. In fact, the business is recording different events. To see where an error could occur, write the asset and expense changes separately from the cash movements.

## A small journal with explicit conventions

The cooperative pays $2,400 for paper on day 0 and holds it as inventory. In this simplified example only, the $1,200 wages paid and incurred on day 14 are period expense rather than inventoriable production cost. The completed order is delivered on day 21 and qualifies for recognition of $4,800 revenue under the example's assumptions. The paper is then consumed. The buyer pays all $4,800 on day 45. No depreciation, other costs, bad debts or taxes are included here. Actual accounting would require evidence of the recognition conditions and applicable standards.

| Date | Debit | Credit | Cash entry |
| --- | --- | --- | ---: |
| 0 | Inventory 2,400 | Cash 2,400 | −2,400 |
| 14 | Wage expense 1,200 | Cash 1,200 | −1,200 |
| 21 | Receivables 4,800 | Revenue 4,800 | 0 |
| 21 | Paper expense 2,400 | Inventory 2,400 | 0 |
| 45 | Cash 4,800 | Receivables 4,800 | +4,800 |

A debit or credit is not automatically a good or bad dollar. Double entry pairs equal amounts on the two sides. Whether each entry increases its account depends on the kind of account. For an asset, such as inventory or receivables, debits increase it and credits decrease it. The table also puts the cash result where you can see it without using accounting language.

## Watch the reporting boundary

At the end of day 21, cumulative recognized revenue less the stated expenses is $1,200. The cash movement so far is −$3,600, and there is a $4,800 receivable. Given these stipulated entries, the difference between earned result and operating cash is that the order is not yet collected. The comparison includes all days from order through day 21; if you choose a narrower period you need to bring in the opening balances.

When collection arrives, receivables fall and cash rises. The job is not sold again. As a check on bookkeeping, add up all debits and credits in the table, then independently sum the signed cash column. The first test checks posting balance. The second asks a different question and is not redundant.

**Teardown.** “The day-45 cash deposit creates $4,800 of new profit.” Find the recognition date already stated and draw the day-45 substitution between assets. Do not conclude that all deposits are revenue or that all expenses occur when suppliers are paid.

**Transfer.** Keep the delivery date and price but collect only $3,000 by day 45. What receivable remains, and what is cumulative cash after day 45? Explain why an outstanding balance is not automatically a loss or a guarantee of future cash.
