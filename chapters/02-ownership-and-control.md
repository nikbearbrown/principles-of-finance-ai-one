# Ownership and Control Are Different

The owner of a small fabrication firm owns every share. A new investor wants 40 new shares. The manager describes the transaction as giving up “40 percent.” That number might matter if there were 100 shares after the issue. There are not. Start with the actual claim before you debate whether the deal is fair.

## Identify what changes

A business can finance itself by making a promise to repay, by selling an ownership claim, or by using retained resources. A loan need not give the lender ordinary ownership; it can still impose important restrictions. Ownership can include rights to vote and to receive amounts remaining after other claims. Exact provisions depend on the form and governing instruments. In this fictional company there are 100 shares initially, all with equal votes and economic rights. It issues 40 new shares of the same class to the investor. Nobody transfers old shares. Ignore taxes, fees and agreements not stated.

The investor holds 40 of 140 shares, about 28.57%. The founder's 100 are unchanged in number and now represent about 71.43%. The firm receives the issue price; it would not necessarily receive money if the investor bought the same shares from an existing holder instead. A company loan could provide cash without that change in the share count, but would add repayment duties. The share math by itself does not tell you which choice is better.

## Make the decision owner accountable

The firm gives a manager a bonus for signing orders by month-end. The manager signs orders with loose payment terms. The target for reported orders rises; cash available for payroll does not. This is an incentive problem: the measure rewards something real but narrower than the result owners need. An independent approval for unusually late payments or a second part of the incentive based on verified collections can make the decision cost visible. Either control has a cost. Overly strict payment terms can exclude good customers, and a collections bonus can penalize a manager for events outside their control. Explain the tradeoff rather than prescribing a universal rule.

**Teardown.** A simulated memo says the founder “gave away 40% of the firm” and the investor “cannot influence governance.” The fraction uses the wrong denominator; the independence claim is unsupported. Under a toy ordinary-vote majority rule the founder can still cast a majority, but other rights or vote thresholds were not given. Keep facts and inference separate.

**Transfer.** Model an alternative in which the investor buys 40 existing shares directly from the founder. How many shares remain outstanding? Which party receives the price? Give one document that would matter before making a governance recommendation and one criterion independent of ownership percentage.
