# Find What the Owner Actually Gets

A company can have valuable equipment and little value left for its ordinary owners. A competing valuation says its shares are worth $45, while a dividend model puts them at $40. Before you judge either figure, find the claim being measured.

## A claim table before a price table

Assume the whole company stops operating and sells all assets. There is one creditor promised $160 and there are no other claims or sale costs. Under this narrow example, the owner gets what remains after paying the creditor but does not put in more cash.

| Company realizable assets | Creditor gets | Owner gets |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $220 | $160 | $60 |
| $120 | $120 | $0 |

The promised payment and the creditor's actual recovery are not identical in the lower state. Owners have more upside in this two-row table and no guaranteed cash in either state. An actual company's priority and ownership rights have to be read from the relevant agreements and law, not imported from this exercise.

## Two ways of estimating a continuing share

In a new standalone illustration, assume common shareholders expect $2 of dividend per share next year, then growth of 3% per year indefinitely, supported by the company's ability to keep paying under the stated scenario. At an 8% required equity return, the simplified constant-growth dividend value is `2/(.08 − .03) = $40`. The start date and rate matter: next year's $2 is not last year's amount, and growth must be below the discount rate for this modeled infinite stream.

Suppose a comparable-company view multiplies estimated **next-year** earnings of $5 per share by a candidate forward price/earnings multiple of 9. This gives $45 per share. Multiples compare a price to a defined accounting measure; a stock's reported earnings are not necessarily its distribution. The multiple assumes comparability in growth, risk, capital needs or other economics. Check date and earnings convention before comparing an annual multiple with a next-year estimate.

The two estimates are not contradictory by logic. They reflect different assumptions. Neither establishes a market price, specifies how much uncertainty surrounds it, or directs a purchase.

**Teardown.** An analyst averages $40 and $45 and calls the result an independent verification. Ask where the assumptions came from. If both were based on the same optimistic forecast, the cross-check may repeat the error.

**Transfer.** With the same next-year dividend and growth forecast but a 9% return requirement, recompute the dividend estimate. Explain why the result changes even if next year's expected dollar dividend does not.
