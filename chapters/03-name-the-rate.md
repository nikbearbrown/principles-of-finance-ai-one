# Name the Rate Before Multiplying

A manager expects cash to grow 6% in a year. The buying power of that cash will not necessarily grow 6%. A currency quote also changed over the same interval. Before interpreting either result, write what a single dollar or unit can buy, when, and in which currency.

## Compare buying power

Use a made-up year with a 6% nominal return and a 4% rise in the price of a representative bundle. Begin with one bundle worth $100 and model $106 at year end. The bundle then costs $104. Dividing gives 106/104, or about 1.01923 bundles. Here the real return is about 1.92%, not precisely the 2 percentage-point difference between the two rates. The difference can be a useful small-rate approximation; the ratio matches the purchasing-power definition used here.

Inflation is a change in the price level of a specified basket, not the change in every price a person faces. A measure taken from a published index must specify geography, basket, dates and index convention. A particular firm's prices or costs can move differently. The [BLS CPI overview](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/) describes a consumer index; it does not provide a price forecast for this case. Rates, financing conditions and currency prices can also move independently of a firm's staged payments. No single forecast is built into the definitions.

## Compare periods and currencies

Suppose a stipulated quote is a 12% **nominal annual rate compounded monthly** with no fees. The rate per month is 1%. Over a year one dollar becomes `1.01^12`, about 1.126825. Thus the effective annual rate in this example is about 12.68%. A loan or savings advertisement can use different conventions or include costs. Ask for the payment and compounding terms rather than forcing every quote into this calculation.

For a separate purchase of 100 euros, the currency quote changes from $1.10 to $1.20 per euro. Multiplying gives $110 then $120. The dollar cost rose even though the supplier did not raise the euro price. Quote direction is part of the input; inverting the units and continuing to multiply tells the wrong story.

**Teardown.** A response says, “A 6% dollar return is still a 6% real return because every dollar is worth a dollar.” That defines the numerator in dollars and ignores what the denominator measures. Rewrite the statement in units of bundles.

**Transfer.** Compute the real gain from an 8% nominal return when the basket rises 6%. Then check your formula if the price basket is unchanged. State which quoted maturity and compounding convention you would need to compare two borrowing offers.
