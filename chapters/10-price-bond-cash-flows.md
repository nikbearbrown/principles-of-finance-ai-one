# A Bond's Coupon Is Not Its Yield

Two bonds say 5% on the cover. One matures in a year and the other in three. Their prices are different when the required yield is 7%. Nothing about the promised coupons changed. What changed is the value of keeping the same promise for different lengths of time.

## Price the promises on paper

A bond is a debt claim; the investor lends to an issuer under stated terms. See [Investor.gov on bonds](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/investing-basics/investment-products/bonds-or-fixed-income-products/bonds) for definitions and a broader risk explanation. For this fictional case, both bonds have $1,000 face value and a 5% annual coupon, cash paid once per year. Assume promised payments occur as written and evaluation is on a coupon date with no accrued amount or fees. Use the same stipulated annual yield of 7% for each. Do not treat it as a universal rate or a promised realized return.

| Bond | End year 1 | End year 2 | End year 3 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| One-year bond | 1,050 | 0 | 0 |
| Three-year bond | 50 | 50 | 1,050 |

The first is worth 1,050/1.07, about $981.31. The second is worth about $947.51 because a greater portion of its cash comes later. At a yield of 5%, both prices are $1,000 on the assumed coupon date. A buyer paying less than $1,000 will receive a higher promised yield than the coupon rate in this case because repayment at face adds to the coupon amounts. The yield is the discount rate that makes the price and promised schedule agree; the coupon is the contractual dollar payment based on face value.

## Avoid turning the model into a promise

The buyer may sell before maturity, the issuer may not fully pay, an early repayment may be allowed by the terms, or reinvestment opportunities may differ. A yield calculation is not evidence those risks went away. With the rest of the inputs unchanged, increasing the rate reduces the PV of each positive receipt. The three-year bond lost more dollars relative to its par price in this matched example. Other securities can differ in coupon pattern and credit risk, so this example does not rank every short and long bond.

**Teardown.** “The three-year bond pays $150 of coupons, so at the same price and risk it is automatically the better purchase.” Those coupon dollars arrive on different dates and the face repayment arrives later too. What holding period and required return does the answer assume?

**Transfer.** Find the price of an otherwise identical two-year bond at 7%. Check that the value falls between the one- and three-year prices under these exact assumptions. Name one input that could break that check when comparing actual issuers.
