# Price the Schedule, Not the Total

The cooperative can buy a binding tool for $1,200 now. The operator expects $1,450 of later net cash. An analyst subtracts the price and reports “value created: $250.” The arithmetic is fine. The cash is expressed at four different dates.

## Make the comparison at one date

Assume the binding tool has a three-year finite life and no closing cash after the last receipt. The following expected incremental amounts are net of the operating costs in the exercise. Use a stipulated 8% rate per year. Alternative: do nothing for zero incremental cash. Taxes, uncertainty and other constraints are ignored in this simple first pass, not declared unimportant.

| Date | Signed incremental cash ($) |
| --- | ---: |
| Today | −1,200 |
| End of year 1 | +300 |
| End of year 2 | +500 |
| End of year 3 | +650 |

Compute `−1200 + 300/1.08 + 500/1.08² + 650/1.08³`. The result is about $22.44, the **net present value** under the case. The time-zero expense is not divided by 1.08; it is already measured at the comparison date. The sign says this sequence beats a zero alternative under the stated basis. It does not say the firm can pay for it today.

## Ask which assumption is carrying the result

Keep the same total $1,450 and swap the first and last receipts. More arrives earlier and the estimated NPV rises to about $68.67. No additional sale is required for that value difference. In another test, keep the original dates and lower the last receipt by $100. This removes only the present value of that $100, not $100 measured today. The original small positive result may not survive. A sensible recommendation should name that exposure.

If you enter a dated schedule in a tool, look up its treatment of time-zero cash. A routine can discount the first input as if it were one period away. An independent check is to set the rate to zero: the original inputs should total $250, and changes in their order cease to matter. Then restore the rate and compare one term by hand.

**Teardown.** “The three positive amounts add to more than cost, so the order is always worth making.” That conclusion ignores timing and the required return. Explain one way the firm could reject the order without changing the stated total.

**Transfer.** Run the $100 final-year loss test. Show the new NPV, and name one observation that would support changing the year-three forecast rather than treating the lower result as an arbitrary pessimistic case.
