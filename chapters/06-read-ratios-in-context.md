# Read Ratios Without Turning Them Into Causes

Two firms have nearly the same products. One earns more cents per sales dollar. The other earns more profit per dollar of owners' capital. Ranking one firm as “better” without naming the question hides the denominator.

## Use comparable measurements

The following are synthetic values in thousands of dollars for the same hypothetical year. Sales and net income are flows over the year. Assets and equity in the first four lines are **average** balances for the same year. Current assets and current liabilities are measured at the same year-end for a separate liquidity ratio.

| Input | Firm A | Firm B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Annual sales | 500 | 800 |
| Annual net income | 40 | 48 |
| Average assets | 250 | 200 |
| Average equity | 125 | 100 |
| Year-end current assets | 100 | 130 |
| Year-end current liabilities | 80 | 100 |

A earns a net margin of 8%; B earns 6%. Divide annual sales by average assets and turnover is 2 for A and 4 for B. Divide average assets by average equity and each has a multiplier of 2. Multiplying the three ratios gives returns on average equity of 32% and 48%. You can check them directly as 40/125 and 48/100. Turnover, not a larger multiplier, accounts for the reversal relative to the net margin ranking within this three-factor identity.

The identity does not tell you why turnover is higher. Inventory timing, sale of equipment, unusually strong sales, leasing arrangements, or a different product mix can drive comparisons. Those are questions for a better case record, not conclusions licensed by division. Likewise a large equity multiplier can reflect more assets financed by claims other than equity. It is not a free way to improve owners' risk-adjusted results.

## Ask what a liquidity ratio cannot see

Current ratios are 1.25 for A and 1.30 for B. Assets classified as current may include inventory and receivables, not just money in the bank. If the question is whether a loan payment clears next Tuesday, check collection and payment dates. A higher snapshot ratio doesn't answer it. Firms can also have different operating cycles; context matters even when the definitions match.

**Teardown.** “B is safer and better managed because its return on equity is bigger.” Separate the observed result from the causal and safety claims. You can say what produces the measured ratio mathematically; you cannot certify risk or identify the manager's contribution from this table.

**Transfer.** Holding A's net income and average assets constant, suppose its average equity is only 100. Recompute the multiplier and return on equity. What might a lender want to know before treating the new return as an improvement?
