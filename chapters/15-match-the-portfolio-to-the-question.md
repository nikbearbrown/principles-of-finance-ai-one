# Match the Portfolio to the Question

Two assets have the same expected return in the analysis. When you combine them, the combined expected return is unchanged and their returns move less across the two modeled states. How can the portfolio be steadier without one asset being steadily better? Inspect which returns occur together.

## A transparent two-state portfolio

Let both teaching states be equally weighted. The asset returns are invented one-period returns. We compare a portfolio starting with equal dollars in A and B with an all-A or all-B portfolio, no costs or interim actions.

| State | Asset A | Asset B | Half in each |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | −8% | +10% | +1% |
| 2 | +12% | −6% | +3% |
| Weighted mean | +2% | +2% | +2% |

For A, the deviations from the mean are −10 and +10 percentage points. For B they are +8 and −8. In either state the product of those deviations is −80 squared percentage points; averaging over these two modeled states gives a covariance of −80 in that scale. Here correlation is exactly negative one. The weights and magnitudes in this example leave some variation: the 50/50 portfolio has a modeled standard deviation of one percentage point using the two equally weighted states as the entire teaching distribution. We do not infer negative-one correlation in a real market.

An all-A position and the 50/50 position have the same modeled mean. The amount of movement differs because weaknesses occur in different states. In a new stress state where both assets lose value, the same weighted portfolio could lose money. Diversification is not a guarantee and asset labels do not establish independent exposures. See [Investor.gov's guide to asset allocation and diversification](https://www.investor.gov/additional-resources/general-resources/publications-research/info-sheets/beginners-guide-asset) for broader context.

## Start with the obligation

Two fictional households hold $1,000. One needs $1,020 next period and cannot postpone the expense. The other has no near-dated bill and can tolerate variation while saving over many years. In the modeled states the equal-weight portfolio produces $1,010 or $1,030. A positive expected outcome of $1,020 is not a promise to hit the first household's bill. The reliable strategy must be assessed on the due date and supported by access to the money. The second household's flexibility changes the decision question; it does not make these two invented states a suitable long-run forecast.

**Teardown.** “A 50/50 portfolio has half the risk of either investment.” The sentence names weights but fails to identify a risk measure and co-movement. Recompute the portfolio returns first and state what the two-state model actually shows.

**Transfer.** Place 40% in A and 60% in B. Find the return in each state and the mean. Then add an unnamed scenario where both assets lose at least 10%, and explain what can and cannot be bounded for the weighted return. Do not decide what a real household should buy.
