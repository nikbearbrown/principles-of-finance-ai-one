# Limit the Exposure You Mean

The cooperative owes a supplier euros. A finance note says it has “hedged 80% of the risk.” It has actually locked a rate on 80% of the euros expected to be needed. If delivery volume changes or the payment date moves, that distinction will matter.

## Measure a simple exposure and response

A fictional invoice of €1,000 is due at a known date. The company's cash is in dollars. It compares doing nothing with an illustrative commitment to buy €800 for $1.13 per euro at the invoice date. Treat the contract as binding on both parties at the stated terms. Ignore fees, default, taxes and settlement problems in the calculation; this does not teach derivative pricing or recommend a transaction. In two teaching states the dollar price of the euro is $1.07 or $1.20.

| Spot price at payment | Dollar cost with no hedge | Dollar cost if €800 are locked |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1.07 per euro | $1,070 | $1,118 |
| $1.20 per euro | $1,200 | $1,144 |

The locked euros cost $904, with the remaining €200 at the state price. The difference between modeled low and high bills falls from $130 to $26. In the lower price state the hedge produces a higher dollar bill than remaining fully exposed. Its job in this scenario is to change the size and direction of exposure, not to yield a cheaper result in every state.

## Check the risk that remains

Suppose the supplier may cancel part of the order. A fixed commitment to buy currency can become larger than the revised need. A wrong date can also leave a gap even when the amounts match. Track the underlying quantity and due date, what the agreement actually delivers, and how the residual will be monitored. Some risks could instead be reduced by changing invoice currency, requiring deposits, insuring a defined loss, or declining the underlying action. Each alternative shifts responsibilities and can cost money. Do not assume a price hedge fixes a delayed shipment or a failed counterparty.

**Teardown.** “The hedge removes 80% of total risk, and lower spot always lowers the final covered bill.” Use the actual dollar payments to state what changed, and name what the posed model does not measure. Lower spot in the example changes the unhedged balance, not the locked term.

**Transfer.** The supplier reduces the confirmed need from €1,000 to €600, but the €800 commitment remains. If the company can sell any excess immediately at the same spot price, calculate net dollar cost in both states. Identify the sign of what is now a surplus exposure, and write a monitoring trigger based on actual invoice size and settlement date.
